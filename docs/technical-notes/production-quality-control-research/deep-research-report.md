# Techniques to Mitigate Selection Bias in a Two-Stage Pipeline

When a cheap classifier in a pipeline only forwards selected documents to an expensive “extractor”, the system suffers **selective-label bias** (also called *survivorship bias*): outcomes are observed only for approved cases, and entire regions of the input space may be unobserved.  This is the classic *selective labels* problem.  In such settings, training the classifier on only those accepted examples can entrench mistakes (e.g. rejecting a whole class of documents) because the classifier never learns from its own rejections.  For example, evaluating a recidivism model only on those granted bail biases performance estimates if denied defendants differ.  Naïve fixes like imputing missing labels (e.g. treating all rejects as “failures”) typically assume **missing-at-random**, which rarely holds in selective-label settings.  

To correct for this bias, several **counterfactual and exploration strategies** have been studied.  Broadly, these include: 

- **Exploration/Exploitation (Bandit) Strategies:**  Introduce randomness into the classifier so that some initially rejected documents are occasionally processed.  This ensures the system “explores” outside the narrow accepted set, collecting outcome feedback in previously unexplored regions.  In bandit terms, one might implement an ε‑greedy or Thompson-sampling scheme: e.g. with small probability ε, override the classifier to send a document through the extractor.  Wei (2021) notes that a **stochastic (exploring) policy** can eventually learn an optimal decision rule, whereas a fixed deterministic policy may get stuck in suboptimal acceptance sets.  In practice, even low-rate random sampling (say, 5–10% of rejects) can break the feedback loop of bias.  Contextual bandit algorithms (e.g. Bootstrap Thompson Sampling or linUCB-style methods) have been proposed for similar online selective-label problems, though they typically assume more data.  

- **Off-Policy/Counterfactual Evaluation:**  Use *logged data* from the existing policy to estimate how a new policy or decision rule would perform.  The key tool is **propensity scoring** (inverse-propensity weighting, IPS).  One treats the historical classifier as a “behavior policy” that generated the training data, and reweights each observed label by the inverse of its selection probability.  For example, if document $i$ had features $X_i$ and selection indicator $T_i$ (1=accepted, 0=rejected), and the classifier’s probability of acceptance was $\pi_b(X_i)$, IPS would weight its outcome $Y_i$ by $1/\pi_b(X_i)$.  In batch learning this idea underlies **Counterfactual Risk Minimization (CRM)**: Swaminathan & Joachims (2015) develop bounds and algorithms for learning from logged bandit feedback by propensity-weighted loss minimization.  Similarly, **Doubly Robust (DR)** estimators combine a propensity-weighted loss with a learned outcome model to reduce variance.  These methods have become standard in off-policy learning.  

- **Causal Reweighting and Sample-Selection Correction:**  From causality, one can view the selection bias as a difference between the *training distribution* and the *target population*.  A common approach is to re-weight the observed data by the inverse probability of being in the sample.  For example, if one could model $P(\text{selected}|X)$ (the selection propensities), one could weight each example by $1/P(\text{selected}|X)$ to recover population-level estimates.  In practice this often requires external data on the target population; when only the “selected” data are available, one must make strong assumptions or use methods like **Heckman correction** or bounding techniques.  Recent work (Hatt et al. 2023) formulates such selection bias explicitly: without separate population samples, they propose uncertainty sets and worst-case (“minimax”) policy evaluation to guard against unknown selection mechanisms.  In our pipeline, if the classifier’s decision rule (e.g. a threshold) is known, one could attempt to infer or model $P(\text{select}=1|X)$ and apply IPS or DR reweighting.  However, a deterministic policy (0/1 selection) yields extreme propensities, so one usually needs to *randomize* or smooth the policy to make weighting feasible.  

- **Selective-Labels Debiasing (Label Imputation or EM):**  Another strategy is to **impute or augment the missing labels** using assumptions or models.  A simple (but often biased) heuristic is to label all unselected items as negatives; Chang & Wiens (2024) describe how treating untested individuals as “no disease” inflates biases.  They propose a **Disparate Censorship EM (DCEM)** algorithm: using an expectation–maximization approach, they iteratively estimate the probability of a positive label for unlabeled instances (based on a model of selection bias) and retrain the classifier.  This kind of EM/pseudo-labeling can mitigate bias if the selection mechanism is somewhat understood.  More generally, semi-supervised or weakly-supervised learning methods (e.g. dataset shift correction, domain adaptation) can use features of the 20K unlabeled “population” to adjust the model.  For example, one could train a model on the labeled set and then apply it to the unlabeled cases, using its confident predictions as pseudo-labels (possibly with manual review).  These techniques introduce model-based assumptions but can provide “surrogate” feedback for the unobserved cases.  

**Viability at Small Scale:**  With only ~440 labeled examples and no live traffic, each approach has trade-offs.  Bandit-style exploration (random sampling) is conceptually simple and can work even with few examples: e.g. setting a modest ε to force occasional labels outside the current decision rule.  Wei (2021) argues stochastic exploration is crucial to avoid lock-in.  However, without many rounds, complex bandit algorithms or heavy hyperparameter tuning are unrealistic.  

Off-policy weighting (IPS/DR) is theoretically appealing, but with so few data points it can suffer from high variance.  Propensity scores estimated from limited data will be noisy, and reweighting can overweight a few rare cases.  Doubly robust methods help by blending in a predictive model of outcomes, which may reduce variance if the extractor’s successes can be modeled well.  One could also use **weight clipping** or **self-normalized IPS** to stabilize the estimates.  In practice, if the cheap classifier’s decision boundary is known, one could **simulate small propensities** by adding a smoothing parameter or by purposely randomizing it.   

Selective-label imputation methods (like DCEM) can be used if one trusts the model assumptions.  But with only 440 labels, a complex EM might overfit.  A pragmatic variant is to manually label a small, diverse set of rejected documents (e.g. those the classifier assigns low confidence or coming from underrepresented sources) and include these in retraining.  Essentially, this is **active learning**: sample the most informative rejects (or a random holdout) for manual extraction.  Even a few dozen new labels can significantly expand the classifier’s perspective.  

**Practical Recommendations:**  In summary, correcting this bias likely requires a **hybrid strategy**: 

- Maintain an epsilon‐greedy policy (e.g. ~5% random selection) to gather counterexamples.  This exploration helps discover if some rejected class actually yields successes.  
- Use **inverse propensity scoring (IPS)** when evaluating or retraining the classifier on the logged data: weight each training example by the inverse of its selection probability (estimated or smoothed).  If possible, combine this with a predictive outcome model (doubly robust) to reduce variance.  
- Consider a lightweight EM or pseudo-labeling approach to assign soft labels to unlabeled items, but only after verifying assumptions (e.g. consult domain experts on likely outcomes of rejected cases).  
- Evaluate model performance **off-policy**: for instance, use counterfactual evaluation or doubly robust estimates to estimate what the classifier’s error would be on the full 20K population.  This helps check if a policy change (e.g. lowering threshold) actually reduces bias.  
- Given small data, avoid overly complex methods.  Clipped IPS or self-normalized estimators (as per CRM) help control variance.  Emphasis should be on **validation**: whenever possible, test the effects of exploration on a held-out set or via cross-validation, rather than purely trusting biased logs.  

Overall, the literature emphasizes that **without exploration, selection bias will persist**.  At this scale, the most viable fixes are simple: inject randomness to get some missing labels, and use propensity-based weighting (with care) to adjust for any remaining bias.  These steps, informed by counterfactual evaluation theory (CRM, IPS, DR), offer the best practical balance between rigor and feasibility in a low-data regime.

Recent work (Chang & Wiens 2024) specifically addresses imputation for selective medical labels; the CRM principle and doubly robust estimators are detailed in Joachims *et al.* (2015). All of the above highlight the need for careful bias correction when training on selectively labeled data.
