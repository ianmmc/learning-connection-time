# Correcting Selection/Survivorship Bias in a Small-Scale Human-in-the-Loop LLM Extraction Pipeline

## TL;DR
- **The single most effective and appropriate intervention for your scale is deliberate, small-percentage random exploration of rejected documents (forward/process 2–5% of what the deterministic classifier rejects and feed the true LLM outcomes back into training).** This is the one technique that actually breaks the feedback loop without heavy statistical machinery, and the most directly analogous recent study (Scarone & Baeza-Yates, ECML PKDD 2026) finds that "even minimal exploration rates (2–5%) prove sufficient... to diagnose the severity of the feedback loop at near-zero cost."
- **Most of the "heavy" literature (large-scale IPS/doubly-robust off-policy evaluation, contextual bandits) is not viable at your scale** (~440 labels, ~20k entities, batch/offline, deterministic legacy logging with no propensity scores) — those methods need logged randomization and thousands-to-millions of interactions. They remain valuable as *conceptual framing* and for *evaluation vocabulary*, not as drop-in estimators.
- **Your best-fit toolkit is: (1) random-exploration sampling of rejects, (2) manual audit of a random sample of rejects to directly measure the false-rejection rate, (3) uncertainty-based active learning to spend your tiny labeling budget well, and (4) monitoring recall/"rejection quality" rather than accuracy — because accuracy is actively misleading under survivorship bias.**

## Key Findings

1. **This is textbook "selective labels."** Your setup is a direct instance of the Selective Labels Problem (Lakkaraju et al., KDD 2017): outcomes (LLM success/failure) are observed only for documents the classifier approved, so the retraining data represents `P(Y | X, D=1)` not `P(Y | X)`. Training naively on it entrenches rejection of whole regions of feature space.

2. **Accuracy will lie to you; recall/rejection-quality is the metric that matters.** The most directly analogous recent empirical study (credit "reject inference," which has the identical feedback structure) shows that under survivorship bias a retrained model's *accuracy stays flat or rises while its recall collapses* — an "illusion of improvement." Standard metrics systematically reward strategies that amplify the bias.

3. **Deterministic legacy decisions largely rule out classical off-policy evaluation.** IPS/doubly-robust estimators require the logging policy to have assigned nonzero probability to every action (the "common support"/positivity assumption). A deterministic classifier violates this, so you have no valid propensity scores to invert. Workarounds exist (treating policy changes over time as implicit randomization, à la Strehl et al. 2010) but are fragile with only ~440 labels.

4. **Econometric selection correction (Heckman) is theoretically relevant but practically weak here.** It needs a credible "exclusion restriction" (a variable affecting selection but not outcome) and is well documented as fragile, collinear, and easily misapplied — a poor fit for a ~440-example document pipeline.

5. **The cheap, robust fix is exploration.** Every strand of the literature — predictive-policing feedback loops (Ensign et al. 2018), recommender degeneration (Jiang et al. 2019), hidden feedback loops (Sculley et al. 2015), counterfactual learning systems (Bottou et al. 2013) — converges on the same prescription: inject deliberate exploration/randomization so the system keeps observing outcomes for cases it would otherwise never see.

6. **Active learning is the right way to spend a tiny labeling budget**, and there is a purpose-built method (SEL-BALD, NeurIPS 2024) for exactly the case where the system declines to label some machine-selected instances.

## Details

### 1. The Selective Labels Problem literature

**Foundational paper.** Himabindu Lakkaraju, Jon Kleinberg, Jure Leskovec, Jens Ludwig, and Sendhil Mullainathan, "The Selective Labels Problem: Evaluating Algorithmic Predictions in the Presence of Unobservables," KDD 2017 (pp. 275–284, DOI 10.1145/3097983.3098066; PMID 29780658). Core idea: when a decision-maker determines which instances get labeled, observed outcomes are a biased, non-random subset, and both evaluation and training are corrupted — worsened by "unobservables" (unmeasured confounders that influence both the decision and the outcome). It is formally a missing-data / censoring problem.

**The "contraction" technique.** Their proposed solution "allows us to compare the performance of predictive models and human decision-makers without resorting to counterfactual inference" by harnessing *heterogeneity among decision-makers*: with multiple decision-makers of varying leniency facing quasi-randomly assigned cases, you start from the most lenient decision-maker's accepted set (most labels) and "contract" it by having the algorithm select additional cases to reject, comparing failure rates across leniency levels. Estimate quality improves with (a) the acceptance rate of the most lenient decision-maker, (b) agreement rate, and (c) number of cases judged by that decision-maker. **Applicability to you: essentially none in its native form** — you have a single deterministic classifier, not a population of heterogeneous human judges with quasi-random assignment. It matters mainly as the canonical framing and as a warning that imputation-based evaluation is over-optimistic.

**Kleinberg, Lakkaraju, Leskovec, Ludwig & Mullainathan, "Human Decisions and Machine Predictions," Quarterly Journal of Economics 133(1):237–293, 2018.** Applies the contraction/leniency idea to NYC bail data, using quasi-random assignment of defendants to judges of differing release rates as a natural experiment. Confirms the general lesson: comparing an algorithm to the decision-maker that generated the data requires confronting the one-sided missingness of labels (we observe crime outcomes only for released defendants).

**Follow-up / partial-identification work.** Point identification of true model performance under selective labels is generally impossible without strong assumptions; recent work studies *partial identification* (bounds) — De-Arteaga et al. 2018; D'Amour 2019; Guo et al. 2022; Yin et al. 2024; and Wei, "Decision-Making Under Selective Labels: Optimal Finite-Domain Policies," ICML 2021.

**De-Arteaga, Dubrawski & Chouldechova, "Leveraging Expert Consistency to Improve Algorithmic Decision Support"** (FAT/ML 2018 workshop version "Learning under selective labels in the presence of expert consistency," arXiv:1807.00905; extended in Management Science 71(12):10465–10485, 2025). Key idea: use historical *expert consistency* (where multiple experts agree) to impute/amalgamate labels — learning from decisions where experts agree and from observed outcomes otherwise. Notably, they argue standard sample-selection-bias corrections often *cannot* fix selective labels even absent unobservables. **Applicability to you: moderate as inspiration** — if your deterministic classifier's decisions can be cross-checked against a human reviewer or a second signal, "learn from agreement, observe outcomes otherwise" is a usable pattern, but the method itself assumes multiple experts.

**SEL-BALD (Ruijiang Gao, Mingzhang Yin & Maytal Saar-Tsechansky, "SEL-BALD: Deep Bayesian Active Learning with Selective Labels," NeurIPS 2024; Best Paper Runner-Up, WITS 2024).** This is the closest published match to your exact problem. It formalizes "Active Learning with Instance Rejection (ALIR)," where the human/system may "decline to label instances selected by the machine learning model due to reasons such as regulation constraint, domain knowledge, or algorithmic aversion." It jointly models the ML classifier *and* a "human discretion" model, and its variants (e-BALD, Joint-BALD, Joint-BALD-UCB, Joint-BALD-TS) add UCB/Thompson-style exploration so the acquisition strategy doesn't get stuck in the high-approval-probability region — precisely the entrenchment failure you describe. Their own experiments show that without added exploration, acquisition "keeps sending such instances to humans... and waste all the budget," failing to recover the correct decision boundary in the under-sampled region. **Applicability: high as a design template**, though it is a deep-Bayesian method that may be heavier than a ~440-example project strictly needs.

### 2. Counterfactual / off-policy evaluation (OPE) from bandits & RL

**Core methods and citations.** Inverse Propensity Scoring (IPS; Horvitz–Thompson 1952 lineage); Self-Normalized IPS (SNIPS; Swaminathan & Joachims 2015); Doubly Robust estimators (Dudík, Langford & Li, "Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Learning," ICML 2011; Dudík, Erhan, Langford & Li, Statistical Science 29(4):485–511, 2014); DR with shrinkage (Su, Dimakopoulou, Krishnamurthy & Dudík, ICML 2020). Core idea: reweight logged outcomes by the inverse probability that the logging policy took the action, to estimate what a *new* policy would have scored. DR combines a reward model ("direct method") with IPS so it is consistent if *either* the propensity model or the reward model is right.

**The fatal obstacle for your setup: no logged propensities and deterministic logging.** IPS/DR require the "common support"/positivity assumption — every action the target policy might take had nonzero probability under the logging policy. A deterministic classifier assigns probability 0 or 1, so importance weights are undefined/infinite for the rejected region. Recent papers confirm deterministic logging causes "severe bias" for standard IPS (e.g., work on OPE for ranking under deterministic logging, and on OPE in deterministic ad auctions) and require special assumptions (exploiting click stochasticity, etc.) to work around it.

**Partial workaround: Strehl, Langford, Li & Kakade, "Learning from Logged Implicit Exploration Data," NeurIPS 2010.** If the deterministic policy *changed over time* (e.g., you retrained the classifier across batches), you can treat the sequence of deterministic policies as implicit randomization and *estimate* propensities `π̂(a|x)` by regression, then do IPS-style correction. **Applicability to you: conceptually possible but statistically thin** with ~440 labels — estimated propensities on tiny data will be noisy, and the technique was validated on large Yahoo! datasets.

**Verdict.** Treat OPE as a *framing and evaluation* discipline, not a production estimator at your scale. It tells you *why* naive retraining is biased and gives you the vocabulary (positivity, importance weighting), but the estimators themselves need randomization and volume you don't have.

### 3. Propensity weighting / Heckman selection correction

**Inverse probability weighting (IPW).** The same math as IPS but framed in the econometrics/epidemiology tradition: reweight observed (accepted) cases by the inverse probability of being accepted to recover the population estimate. Refinements include weight truncation, weight stabilization, and augmented IPW. Same positivity problem: rejected regions with acceptance probability ≈ 0 get near-infinite weights.

**Heckman correction (Heckman two-step / "Heckit," 1976/1979; Heckman won the 2000 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for this line of work).** Step 1: a probit selection equation models the probability of being selected (accepted), yielding the Inverse Mills Ratio (IMR). Step 2: include the IMR as a regressor in the outcome equation to correct the bias. In credit scoring this appears as the bivariate-probit reject-inference approach. **Applicability to you: weak.** Method reviews (e.g., Bushway, Johnson & Slocum, "Is the Magic Still There? The Use of the Heckman Two-Step Correction for Selection Bias in Criminology," Journal of Quantitative Criminology 2007) document that Heckman is routinely misapplied, is fragile to its normality assumption, produces inflated/collinear standard errors, and critically *requires an exclusion restriction* — a variable that affects whether a document is forwarded but not whether extraction succeeds. In a document pipeline such a variable is hard to justify, and with ~440 labels the two-stage estimator would be badly underpowered. Useful to know, unlikely to deploy.

**Reject inference (the credit-scoring literature = your closest industrial analog).** Techniques: extrapolation/hard-cutoff augmentation, parceling, fuzzy augmentation, reweighting/augmentation, bivariate (Heckman) models, and semi-supervised approaches (safe semi-supervised SVM; self-learning; graph-based). **Crucial cautionary finding:** empirical scorecard studies show little evidence these imputation methods reliably improve performance, and Scarone & Baeza-Yates (2026, below) show extrapolation can actively *reverse the sign* of the bias. The lesson: **imputing labels for rejects is dangerous; observing true labels for a sample of rejects is safe.**

### 4. Explore/exploit and active learning (small-data regime)

**Bandit exploration.** ε-greedy (choose best action with prob 1−ε, random otherwise), UCB (optimism under uncertainty; Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer 2002), and Thompson sampling (Bayesian posterior sampling; Thompson 1933) are the canonical strategies. In practice ε-greedy is favored for being "computationally inexpensive and straightforward" despite weaker theoretical guarantees than UCB/Thompson. **For your pipeline, ε-greedy maps directly and cleanly onto "randomly forward a small ε-fraction of rejected documents."** Full contextual-bandit machinery (LinUCB etc.) is built for millions of users/offers and is overkill.

**Active learning (the right tool for ~440 labels).** Burr Settles, "Active Learning Literature Survey" (2009/2012) is the standard reference. Pool-based uncertainty sampling (Lewis & Gale 1994) — label the unlabeled instances the current model is least certain about (least-confidence, margin, or entropy) — is "the simplest and most commonly used" strategy and is explicitly designed to reach higher accuracy with fewer labels. Query-by-committee (Seung et al. 1992) and diversity/representativeness criteria are complements. **Applicability: high.** With only ~440 labels and ~20k entities, uncertainty sampling is the most cost-effective way to choose your next labeling batch. **Important caveat specific to your problem:** naive uncertainty sampling can *reinforce* the survivorship bias because it samples near the current (biased) decision boundary and may never probe the confidently-rejected region — which is exactly why SEL-BALD adds exploration, and why you should combine active learning with *random* exploration of rejects rather than relying on uncertainty sampling alone.

**Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning** (Elkan & Noto, KDD 2008; du Plessis et al. 2014; Kiryo et al. 2017; survey Bekker & Davis 2020). Learns a classifier from positive + unlabeled data under the assumption labeled positives are a random subset of all positives (the "SCAR" assumption). Potentially relevant if you treat "forwarded & succeeded" as positive and everything else as unlabeled, but SCAR is violated by a deterministic classifier, so treat PU learning as a secondary option.

### 5. Runaway feedback loops in self-training ML systems

- **Sculley et al., "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems," NIPS 2015** (Google). Names "hidden feedback loops" and "direct feedback loops" as core ML-specific technical-debt risks: systems that influence their own future training data. Prescription includes isolating/monitoring these loops and using randomization to keep them measurable.
- **Ensign, Friedler, Neville, Scheidegger & Venkatasubramanian, "Runaway Feedback Loops in Predictive Policing," FAT* 2018 (PMLR 81:160–171).** Proves mathematically why a system that only observes outcomes where it acted spirals (police sent to the same neighborhoods regardless of true crime rate); shows the fix is to change the inputs so discovered incidents are down-weighted by how much you sampled that region — i.e., a propensity-style correction for self-generated data. Direct structural analog to your "entrench rejection of a whole class" concern.
- **Jiang, Chiappa, Lattimore, György & Kohli, "Degenerate Feedback Loops in Recommender Systems," AAAI/ACM AIES 2019** (DeepMind). Shows recommender interest/serving degenerates ("echo chambers") under both UCB and Thompson serving, and that the controllable lever is the *speed* of degeneration; random exploration and maintaining candidate diversity slow it.
- **Bottou, Peters, Quiñonero-Candela, Charles, Chickering, Portugaly, Ray, Simard & Snelson, "Counterfactual Reasoning and Learning Systems," JMLR 14, 2013** (Microsoft/Bing). The industrial blueprint for deliberately injecting randomization (small controlled perturbations) into a production system so you can later answer counterfactual "what if the policy had been different" questions with importance sampling. The philosophical foundation for "log your exploration."
- **D'Amour, Srinivasan, Atwood, Baljekar, Sculley & Halpern, "Fairness Is Not Static: Deeper Understanding of Long-Term Fairness via Simulation Studies," FAT* 2020** (Google; ML-fairness-gym). Demonstrates via simulation that static metrics miss feedback dynamics and that you must simulate/monitor the loop over time.
- **Scarone & Baeza-Yates, "The Illusion of Improvement: Reject Inference Strategies in Credit Scoring," arXiv:2606.18479, accepted ECML PKDD 2026.** The most directly transferable recent result — see below.

### 6. What is actually viable at your scale — concrete assessment

The single most relevant empirical evidence comes from **Bruno Scarone (Northeastern University) & Ricardo Baeza-Yates (KTH Royal Institute of Technology), "The Illusion of Improvement" (ECML PKDD 2026)**, which simulates exactly your loop (a classifier that filters cases, retrained over 10 iterations on outcomes observed only for approved cases). Note: it frames the problem as "survival bias" / performative prediction and cites Ensign et al. (2018), but does *not* itself cite Lakkaraju et al. 2017 — I connect the two literatures here.

- **Failure mode confirmed:** under the biased loop (Default dataset, gradient-boosted trees, initial training set 5,000), over 10 cycles "Accuracy remains stable and even increases slightly, rising from approximately 0.820 to 0.835... this apparent stability masks a collapse in recall, from approximately 0.37 to 0.13." The training set's true base rate deflated from the population's 22.0% to ~17.1% (a ~5-point, −22%-relative distortion). They call this "the illusion of improvement," and conclude standard metrics "systematically reward strategies that amplify survival bias."
- **Imputation is worse than nothing:** "Simple Extrapolation" (impute reject labels from the model) *inflated* the base rate the opposite direction (from 22.0% to ~26.8%, +22% relative) and "consistently outperformed the Oracle" on accuracy while being substantively wrong. Their verdict: "Extrapolation does not mitigate survival bias; it reverses its sign, replacing systematic underrepresentation of defaulters with systematic overrepresentation."
- **The fix — controlled exploration:** "the lender deliberately approves a fraction r of rejected applicants and observes their true repayment outcomes. This controlled exploration... breaks the feedback loop at a measurable cost." They sweep r ∈ {0.01, 0.02, 0.05, ..., 1.0} and three selection rules (Least Risky, Most Risky, Random), using *observed* (not imputed) labels — making it assumption-free.
- **How much:** "Even minimal exploration rates (2–5%) prove sufficient in our experiments to diagnose the severity of the feedback loop at near-zero cost." They define a "diagnostic zone at r ∈ [0.01, 0.05]" where at r=0.05 "accuracy changes are negligible across all configurations (|∆| < 0.4%)" yet the bias becomes visible. Explicit recommendation: "A short-term diagnostic experiment at r = 0.02–0.05, using random selection among rejected applicants, is sufficient to estimate the [base-rate] gap and quantify the severity of the feedback loop." (On their dataset, r=0.02 across all iterations incurred ~45 additional bad outcomes — the literal "near-zero cost.")
- **Key strategic insight for you:** "A bank that evaluates its scoring model by standard metrics will rationally choose r = 0, because exploration can only decrease accuracy on the biased test distribution. This is the optimal strategy under the metric the bank is optimizing, and the worst possible strategy for learning." In other words, *if you judge your pipeline by observed accuracy you will never explore, and you will slowly go blind.*

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Instrument and diagnose (do first).**
1. **Switch your primary health metric from accuracy to recall / "rejection quality."** Under survivorship bias accuracy is not just uninformative but actively misleading (Scarone & Baeza-Yates). Track the estimated false-rejection rate over retraining cycles.
2. **Run a one-off diagnostic exploration at r = 2–5%:** for a fixed period, randomly forward 2–5% of documents the deterministic classifier would reject, send them to the LLM extractor, and log the true success/failure. This is the literature's near-zero-cost diagnostic. With ~20k entities, a 2–5% reject-exploration sample is a few hundred documents — well matched to your budget.
3. **Manually audit a random sample of rejects** (independent of the LLM path) to get a second, unbiased estimate of how many good documents are being wrongly rejected, and of *which class* is being systematically excluded.

**Stage 2 — Institutionalize exploration (ongoing).**
4. **Make reject-exploration permanent as an ε-greedy policy** (e.g., ε ≈ 2–5% of rejects forwarded at random each batch), and **log the exploration decision** so every future retraining set contains outcomes from the rejected region. This is the Bottou et al. / Sculley et al. discipline: keep the loop observable.
5. **Add uncertainty-sampling active learning** on top, to direct your scarce human labeling toward the most informative instances — but draw part of each active-learning batch from the *rejected* region specifically, so uncertainty sampling doesn't just polish the existing boundary (the SEL-BALD lesson). If you want to probe where the classifier has the least information, "Most Risky"/highest-rejection-confidence sampling surfaces the excluded class fastest, at higher outcome cost; random sampling gives an unbiased estimate.

**Stage 3 — Correct and re-evaluate.**
6. **Retrain the classifier on the union of accepted-outcome data + explored-reject outcomes + audited labels.** Because the explored rejects carry *observed* (not imputed) labels, this is assumption-free bias correction. **Do not use extrapolation/parceling-style label imputation** — the evidence says it can worsen the bias.
7. **If you later want a quantitative bias estimate**, use the exploration sample's known sampling rate as a genuine propensity (you now control it) to compute simple IPW-corrected performance estimates — this is the *only* clean way to get valid importance weights in your system, and it exists precisely because you added randomization.

**Thresholds that should change the plan:**
- If the diagnostic exploration shows the false-rejection rate is *negligible* (explored rejects almost never succeed in extraction), you can lower ε toward ~1% and rely mostly on monitoring.
- If exploration reveals a *systematically excluded class* (a cluster of rejects the LLM actually handles well), escalate: temporarily raise ε for that stratum, or hard-route that class to the LLM until the classifier is retrained.
- If your project ever grows to high-volume online serving with logged randomization, *then* graduate to SNIPS/doubly-robust OPE and contextual bandits — not before.

## Caveats
- **Evidence transfer is by analogy.** Most direct empirical validation comes from criminal justice (bail), credit scoring, predictive policing, and recommenders — not document-extraction pipelines. The *structure* (self-generated labels, one-sided missingness) is identical, but exact numbers (e.g., the 2–5% exploration figure) come from credit-scoring simulations and should be validated on your own data.
- **The Scarone & Baeza-Yates (2026) figures are the authors' own single-preprint simulation results on three public datasets**, several read off plots ("approximately"). Treat them as directional, not definitive. The paper is peer-reviewed (accepted ECML PKDD 2026) but not independently replicated, and it does not itself engage the Lakkaraju/selective-labels line of work by name — the bridge between the two literatures is my synthesis.
- **Exploration has real cost.** Forwarding rejected documents to an expensive LLM costs compute/money; in high-stakes settings deliberately "approving" would-be-rejects raises ethical/regulatory questions. At your batch/offline scale these costs are modest, which is exactly why exploration is affordable for you and not for a bank processing millions of loans.
- **Deterministic legacy data cannot be retroactively fixed.** No estimator recovers unbiased information from a region that was never sampled; the correction only works going forward once you introduce randomization. Historical rejects can only be addressed by auditing/labeling them now.
- **Small n means wide error bars.** With ~440 labels every estimate (false-reject rate, retrained performance) will be noisy; prefer simple, robust procedures (random sampling, direct audit) over multi-parameter models (Heckman, deep Bayesian) that are easy to overfit at this scale.
- **Conflicting evidence on imputation.** The credit-scoring industry still widely uses reject-inference imputation, but the academic evidence (Scarone & Baeza-Yates 2026; earlier scorecard studies) is that it is unreliable or harmful. I side with the observe-true-labels-via-exploration approach; flagging the disagreement explicitly.
