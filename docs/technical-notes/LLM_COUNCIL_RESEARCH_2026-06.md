## TL;DR — findings that shaped our council design

1. **Diversity > count, measured by model *family*.** Across 350+ LLMs, two wrong models agree on the *same* wrong answer ~60% of the time (vs ~33% chance); same provider/architecture → more correlated, and *more accurate models are more correlated even across families* ("algorithmic monoculture"). Consensus is only meaningful between **different families**.
2. **2–4 heterogeneous models is the sweet spot.** 1→3 diverse experts = large gain; 3 copies of one model ≈ single model; "more models always better" is folklore — weak/correlated members drag down a majority vote.
3. **Majority vote is a baseline, not best practice.** Its signature failure is **false consensus** (all agree because all share a blind spot). Prefer weighted aggregation or a **judge model** that re-reads the document and reasons about candidates (Council Mode judge: −35.9% hallucination; replacing it with majority vote: +32.7%).
4. **Cascades work for extraction** — FrugalGPT matched the best single model at up to −98% cost; UCCI (production NER) −31% cost at F1 0.91 — but only with **calibrated** confidence routing and when difficulty varies (many easy cases). Within-district schools cluster, so a cascade fits.
5. **Audit which models formed each consensus** — needed to detect monoculture/false consensus after the fact.

**Our application:** cross-family-only agreement (±15 min) for the cheap accept path; a judge model (re-read page + plausibility gate) to resolve disagreements rather than adding more voters; ~3 diverse voters + 1 judge, not two full trios. Family buckets in our 6-model set: Google (Flash, Flash-Lite), Mistral (Small 24B, Large 2512), DeepSeek (V3.2), Qwen (235B-2507).

# Ensemble and Council Architectures for Multi-LLM Structured Data Extraction (2024–2026)

Recent work between 2024 and 2026 has moved beyond single-model large language model (LLM) systems toward *councils* or *ensembles* of models, especially for reliability-critical applications such as structured data extraction from documents. Across e-commerce attribute extraction, document information extraction, financial KPI parsing, and general question answering, the central empirical pattern is that carefully designed ensembles of heterogeneous LLMs can improve accuracy, robustness, and calibration, while cascades and routing policies can simultaneously reduce cost dramatically.[1][4][5][11][14][19] However, the details matter. Naïve majority voting over highly correlated models delivers only modest gains and can even entrench shared biases; in contrast, methods that emphasize architectural diversity, learned or structured aggregation, and calibrated routing achieve consistently better tradeoffs.[3][8][10][11][14] For structured extraction tasks in particular, the most successful contemporary systems combine three ideas: heterogeneous expert models whose errors are as decorrelated as possible; consensus mechanisms that go beyond simple majority voting toward weighted or judge-based aggregation; and cascades or triage that allocate expensive multi-model deliberation only to difficult, uncertain, or disputed cases.[1][4][5][11][14][19] The evidence base is still evolving, but several robust findings now distinguish what reliably works from folklore, and they provide concrete guidance for designing practical multi-LLM councils for structured data extraction.

## 1. Structured Data Extraction with LLM Councils: Scope and Motivation

### 1.1 From open-ended generation to structured extraction

Structured data extraction refers to tasks where the output is not free-form text but a set of fields, labels, spans, or relations that conform to a schema: product attributes, named entities, key–value pairs from invoices, entities and relations in contracts, or financial KPIs from filings.[1][14][19] In such settings, the system must map complex, often noisy natural-language documents to structured representations whose correctness is objectively checkable. This stands in contrast to open-ended generation or narrative summarization, where multiple outputs may be acceptable and evaluation is more subjective.

LLMs are increasingly used as primary engines for document understanding and extraction, performing tasks that once required specialized models for named entity recognition, table extraction, and relation classification.[1][14][19] However, single LLMs still hallucinate, misinterpret subtle context, or miss rare attributes, and their errors can be brittle with respect to small distribution shifts. The need for robustness, combined with the high cost of large models, has led practitioners to explore *multi-model* architectures: councils of LLMs that deliberate, ensembles that vote or aggregate, and cascades that route queries from cheaper to stronger models based on estimated difficulty.[1][4][5][11][14][19]

In structured extraction, the appeal of councils is particularly strong because outputs can be directly compared across models. Unlike open-ended essays, extracted fields can be normalized and matched, enabling explicit notions of agreement, confidence, and conflict.[3][14] This makes it natural to adapt ideas from classical ensemble learning and crowdsourced label aggregation, such as majority vote, Dawid–Skene weighting, and dependence-aware aggregation, to the multi-LLM context.[10][13] Recent work on LLM-ensemble for e-commerce attribute extraction, ZeroSemble for document-level information extraction, and multi-agent financial KPI systems illustrates how structured extraction is becoming a primary testbed for multi-LLM councils.[1][14][19]

### 1.2 Types of multi-LLM architectures in use

By 2026, several architectural patterns have emerged for combining multiple LLMs in structured tasks. The first is the *parallel ensemble*, where multiple models are run in parallel on the same input and their outputs are aggregated via rules, learned weights, or a dedicated judge model. LLM-ensemble for e-commerce attribute extraction falls squarely in this category, iteratively learning optimal weights to combine model predictions for each attribute.[1] ZeroSemble similarly runs a small set of heterogeneous LLMs and then fuses their extractions into a high-confidence entity set before performing relation extraction.[14]

The second pattern is the *multi-agent council*, where models play distinct roles and critique or refine each other’s outputs. Council Mode exemplifies this approach, routing complex queries to a panel of heterogeneous frontier LLMs whose answers are then synthesized by a consensus model that explicitly reasons about agreements, disagreements, and unique findings.[11] Similarly, the llm-council architecture implements a three-stage flow of first opinions, peer review, and final synthesis inspired by human deliberative structures.[2] Although much of this work has focused on question answering and factual reasoning, the same pattern is starting to be applied to structured extraction pipelines.

The third pattern is the *cascade* or *routing* architecture, where a cheap or small model handles easy queries and only difficult, ambiguous, or low-confidence cases are escalated to larger models or multi-model councils.[4][5][11][15] FrugalGPT introduced a paradigm in which an LLM cascade learns which model or combination of models to invoke per query to reduce cost while preserving quality.[5] UCCI extended this idea to a production named entity recognition workload, calibrating token-level uncertainty to error probabilities and using cost-constrained threshold policies to decide when to route to a larger model.[4] In Council Mode, an initial triage classifier decides which queries require the full council, further emphasizing cascaded decision-making.[11]

### 1.3 Why councils matter more for structured extraction

In structured extraction, the tolerance for error is often lower than in open-ended generation. Mis-extracting a product size, interest rate, or drug dose can have direct downstream consequences; failures are not merely stylistic but factual.[1][14][17][19] At the same time, structured outputs lend themselves to systematic post-processing, validation, and logical consistency checks, which can be integrated into a council’s decision logic. Extraction tasks also often involve recurring schemas across large volumes of documents, enabling data-driven learning of ensemble weights, routing policies, and uncertainty calibrations.[1][4][5][14]

As a result, many of the empirically successful council designs for structured extraction combine statistical ensemble methods with domain-specific constraints. LLM-ensemble learns model weights on historical attribute extraction data and then uses them to aggregate future predictions.[1] ZeroSemble constructs a high-confidence entity set and then uses it to constrain allowed relations, effectively injecting structural priors into the multi-LLM pipeline.[14] Financial KPI extraction systems integrate LLM extraction agents with rule-based validation and schema-aware text-to-SQL agents, forming modular multi-agent pipelines where LLM outputs are checked and transformed before final use.[19] These examples illustrate that for structured extraction, councils are not just about redundancy; they are about explicitly modeling reliability, exploiting schema knowledge, and routing effort where it matters most.

With this context, the following sections address the user’s five specific questions, drawing on the burgeoning literature on model diversity, consensus mechanisms, cascades, ensemble scaling, and disagreement handling, with particular attention to structured data extraction.

## 2. Model Diversity and Error Correlation in Multi-LLM Ensembles

### 2.1 The ensemble intuition and the role of independence

Classical ensemble learning theory establishes that combining multiple base learners can reduce variance and improve accuracy, especially when individual learners are better than random and their errors are not too strongly correlated. In the simplest setting of binary classification, if each model has error rate \(p < 0.5\) and errors are independent, a majority vote over \(N\) models yields an ensemble error that drops exponentially with \(N\). This relies crucially on independence: if all models fail on the same examples, majority voting provides no benefit. For structured extraction tasks, often framed as multi-label classification or span detection, similar intuitions apply. If different models make different mistakes on which fields they mis-extract or hallucinate, then aggregating their predictions can identify consensus on correct fields and treat outliers as unreliable.

However, LLMs are not arbitrary base learners; they are trained on overlapping corpora, share architectures, and alignments, and may be served by the same providers. This raises the question of how independent their errors really are. Recent empirical work has begun to quantify the extent of error correlation and its consequences for ensemble design. These findings strongly influence best practices for model diversity in LLM councils.

### 2.2 Empirical evidence on correlated errors

One of the most comprehensive analyses of error correlation across LLMs is provided by the 2025 study on correlated errors in large language models.[8] The authors conduct large-scale experiments across more than 350 LLMs on two popular leaderboards and a resume-screening task, measuring how often different models make the same mistakes on the same items.[8] They find substantial correlation in model errors: on one HELM dataset, pairs of models agree about 60% of the time when both are incorrect, far higher than the one-third agreement rate that would be expected if they were choosing among incorrect answers uniformly at random.[8] This indicates that, in practice, LLMs often fail on the same examples in similar ways.

Through regression analysis, the study identifies several factors that drive error correlation. Models from the same provider, built on the same base architecture, or with similar sizes tend to exhibit higher agreement in their mistakes.[8] More strikingly, even after conditioning on these observable factors, pairs of models that are more accurate individually also have more correlated errors.[8] In other words, as models improve, they not only converge in overall performance but also in the specific inputs on which they fail. The authors interpret this as a form of algorithmic monoculture: newer, stronger models, possibly due to similar training data and alignment techniques, increasingly behave similarly, including in their blind spots.[8]

These findings have direct implications for multi-LLM councils. They suggest that simply combining a set of top-tier models from the same provider or family will likely yield limited error decorrelation. While such ensembles may still reduce variance in generation and smooth out idiosyncratic responses, their ability to correct systematic extraction errors is constrained by shared blind spots. Designers must therefore seek *diversity* not only in random seeds or prompts but in providers, architectures, and training regimes if they want to meaningfully decorrelate errors.

### 2.3 Heterogeneous ensembles for structured extraction

Several works in structured extraction explicitly embrace model diversity as a design principle. ZeroSemble, a zero-shot document-level information extraction method, uses three cutting-edge LLMs from different families to construct a heterogeneous ensemble for entity and relation extraction.[14] Without any domain-specific training, ZeroSemble first combines entity extractions from these models to produce a high-confidence entity set and then constrains relation extraction based on that set.[14] The authors show that this heterogeneous ensemble can successfully handle document-level information extraction tasks, outperforming single-model baselines.[14] While they do not systematically quantify error correlation, their emphasis on heterogeneity and their empirical gains align with the notion that diverse models capture complementary signals, especially in challenging document contexts.

Council Mode takes this further by explicitly requiring expert diversity. Instead of using multiple instances of the same model, Council Mode queries *heterogeneous frontier LLMs* from different providers in parallel before synthesizing their responses through a dedicated consensus model.[11] In ablation studies, replacing heterogeneous experts with three instances of the same model leads to only modest improvements over the single model, whereas the diverse council yields substantial reductions in hallucination and bias.[11] This confirms empirically that real gains in reliability arise not from sheer redundancy but from cognitive diversity among experts.

The LLM-ensemble method for e-commerce attribute extraction, deployed on Walmart’s internal dataset, also leverages multiple models, though the paper focuses more on the weighting algorithm than on explicit architectural diversity.[1] Nevertheless, the method’s key assumption is that different LLMs make different mistakes on attribute labels, and that learning optimal weights over their outputs can approximate a theoretical optimum under certain error models.[1] Given the findings on correlated errors, practitioners deploying such systems in production increasingly seek to mix models from different sources to maximize the potential benefits of weighted ensemble methods.

In financial document processing, multi-agent systems for extracting and querying KPIs similarly use distinct models for extraction and text-to-SQL translation, with rule-based validation in between.[19] While this is a form of functional rather than architectural diversity, it reinforces the idea that combining models with different strengths and roles can reduce systematic failure modes, particularly when domain-specific validation is available.

### 2.4 Measuring independence and diversity in practice

To move beyond intuition, one needs concrete metrics to assess how independent different LLMs’ errors are on a given extraction workload. The correlated errors study uses simple yet informative measures such as the conditional agreement rate: for pairs of models, the fraction of examples where both are wrong and agree on the same incorrect label.[8] This can be adapted to structured extraction by looking at field-level decisions: for each field or entity, one can compute how often both models fail and produce the same incorrect value or omission.

More sophisticated approaches draw on the crowdsourcing literature on label aggregation. Methods such as the Dawid–Skene model treat each annotator as having a confusion matrix with respect to the latent true label and estimate these matrices jointly with the true labels.[13] Dependence-aware label aggregation extends this by explicitly modeling dependencies among annotators, capturing correlated errors that violate the assumption of independent noise.[10] In the context of multi-LLM councils, one can treat each model as an annotator, estimate their confusion patterns on a labeled subset of data, and even incorporate dependence structures to avoid overcounting repeated mistakes from correlated models.[10]

In practice, diversity metrics may include pairwise disagreement rates on labeled data, clusterings of models based on their error patterns, or mutual information between models’ predictions and ground truth. Even in unlabeled settings, one can approximate diversity by measuring how often models’ structured outputs differ after normalization, though this conflates genuine disagreement with different but equivalent representations. For structured extraction, where fields are discrete and comparable, such metrics are more straightforward than in open-ended generation.

Importantly, measuring diversity is not just academic. It guides model selection: if two models are highly correlated in their errors on a validation set, adding both to a council yields less marginal benefit than adding a third model with a different error profile. It also informs weighting schemes: dependence-aware aggregation can down-weight models that are both less accurate and highly correlated with others, thereby mitigating their drag on ensemble performance.[10][13]

### 2.5 Does mixing model families actually reduce correlated mistakes?

The evidence to date supports a nuanced answer. On one hand, the correlated errors study demonstrates that models with shared providers, architectures, or sizes are more correlated in their errors, implying that mixing models across these dimensions should reduce correlation.[8] Council Mode’s experiments directly show that using heterogeneous models from different providers produces substantially larger reductions in hallucination and bias than using multiple copies of the same model.[11] ZeroSemble’s success with three cutting-edge heterogeneous models in document-level extraction further suggests that such heterogeneity captures complementary perspectives valuable for complex information extraction.[14]

On the other hand, the same study finds that more accurate models are inherently more correlated, even when they differ in provider and architecture.[8] This implies that simply mixing top-tier models will not guarantee low correlation; there is a structural convergence in the kinds of hard cases that all strong models struggle with. In high-stakes settings, this phenomenon underlies the risk of algorithmic monoculture: an apparently strong consensus among powerful models may mask a shared blind spot, leading to a false sense of security.[8]

For structured extraction, the practical takeaway is that mixing model families does reduce correlated mistakes relative to homogeneous ensembles, but the reduction is partial, not complete. The best performing systems therefore combine heterogeneity with additional safeguards: structured consensus mechanisms that explicitly surface disagreements and unique findings, domain-specific validation checks, and, in some cases, human review for flagged items.[11][14][19] Moreover, when learning ensemble weights or routing policies, practitioners should use labeled data to empirically verify that added models provide incremental value, rather than assuming that more diversity is always beneficial. The next sections examine how different consensus mechanisms exploit—or fail to exploit—this limited diversity.

## 3. Consensus Mechanisms: Majority Voting, Weighting, Judge Models, and Self-Consistency

### 3.1 Simple majority voting: strengths and limitations

Majority voting is perhaps the most straightforward ensemble decision rule: multiple model responses are collected, and the most common answer is selected as the final output.[3] In the context of LLMs, this can be implemented either across models—querying different LLMs with the same prompt—or across runs of the same model with different random seeds or prompts. For structured data extraction, majority voting typically involves normalizing model outputs to a canonical representation and then counting how often each distinct value or label occurs.[3][14]

A comprehensive guide on majority voting with LLMs highlights its appeal as a practical, explainable strategy to reduce randomness and increase robustness.[3] When models or prompts are deliberately diversified, majority voting has been shown to improve output consistency and reduce single-run errors in many production systems.[3] The method is particularly effective for constrained tasks where the decision space is well-defined, such as classification or selecting among a set of candidate labels, which aligns well with many extraction subtasks such as attribute classification or entity type assignment.[1][14]

However, the same guide emphasizes several limitations that become critical in multi-LLM councils. First, majority voting can reinforce common biases across models, especially when ensemble members are homogeneous or trained on similar data, leading to correlated failures.[3] Second, it is brittle when voting is applied over unnormalized outputs: minor formatting differences or lexical variations can prevent equivalent answers from being counted together, causing unstable votes.[3] Third, majority voting struggles with highly subjective tasks, but even in structured extraction it may fail when the majority is consistently wrong on edge cases, such as rare attributes or nuances in legal or financial language.[3] Finally, without tie-breaking and confidence-aware mechanisms, majority voting cannot differentiate between strong and weak majorities, leading to overconfident decisions in ambiguous cases.[3]

Empirically, Council Mode provides an instructive comparison. When the authors replace their structured synthesis protocol with simple majority voting over expert answers, the hallucination rate increases by 32.7%, demonstrating that majority vote alone fails to exploit the nuanced information in expert disagreements and quality signals.[11] This is not a structured extraction benchmark per se, but the failure mode is analogous: a pure count of labels ignores model-specific reliability, disagreement structure, and evidence supporting each candidate extraction.

### 3.2 Weighted voting and learned aggregation

Weighted voting extends majority voting by assigning different weights to ensemble members or their outputs, reflecting their reliability or expertise. In classical ensemble learning and crowdsourced label aggregation, methods like Dawid–Skene assign per-annotator confusion matrices and derive posterior label estimates that weigh annotators according to their inferred accuracy.[13] In LLM councils, weighted aggregation can be learned from labeled data or inferred from unlabeled agreement patterns.

The LLM-ensemble algorithm for e-commerce attribute extraction is an archetypal example of learned weighting in a structured extraction setting.[1] The authors propose a novel algorithm called LLM-ensemble that iteratively learns weights for different LLMs to aggregate their label predictions into a final attribute value.[1] Under certain assumptions, they show that their weighting scheme achieves theoretical optimality, and they empirically demonstrate that, on Walmart’s internal dataset, LLM-ensemble outperforms all individual LLMs and state-of-the-art baselines for attribute value extraction.[1] Crucially, the method treats each model’s output as a noisy label and learns from data which models are more reliable for which attributes, integrating this into a weighted aggregation rule rather than relying on an unweighted majority.

ZeroSemble also incorporates an implicit weighting mechanism by constructing a high-confidence entity set based on agreement patterns across heterogeneous LLMs.[14] While the paper does not frame this as a formal weighting model, the two-step pipeline first aggregates entity extractions and then uses the resulting high-confidence set to constrain relation extraction, effectively privileging entities that are supported by multiple models and discarding low-confidence ones.[14] This is conceptually similar to weighted voting in that it emphasizes consensus among reliable models, although it is implemented via thresholding and constraints rather than explicit continuous weights.

More formally, recent work on ensembles of LLMs proposes an AdaBoost-inspired method in which an ensemble of weak supervisors is leveraged to enhance the performance of stronger LLMs across classification and generative tasks.[12] The authors introduce an easy-to-hard framework where weak models trained on simpler tasks collaboratively supervise stronger models on more complex tasks, using a boosting-style procedure that assigns higher weights to examples that previous models misclassified.[12] Their ensemble approach achieves up to 14% improvement over existing baselines and, in several cases, matches the performance of models trained on ground-truth data.[12] While this work focuses more on training a stronger model than on inference-time aggregation, it exemplifies how weighting and adaptive focusing on hard cases can turn an ensemble of imperfect models into a stronger system.

Dependence-aware label aggregation extends the idea of weighting by modeling dependencies between annotators (or models).[10] Instead of assuming that each annotator’s errors are independent given the true label, the method explicitly captures correlations in their judgments, improving aggregation performance over classical baselines on real-world datasets.[10] Translated to LLM councils, this suggests that weighting schemes should consider not only individual model accuracies but also their joint error structures, down-weighting clusters of highly correlated models to avoid overcounting their shared mistakes.

In structured extraction, the practical advantage of weighted aggregation is that it can be tailored to specific fields, document types, or domains. Models that excel at numeric fields might be given more weight on monetary amounts, while others might be preferred for unstructured descriptive attributes. Learning such weights requires labeled data, but extraction pipelines often have access to historical ground truth, at least for a subset of documents, making this feasible.[1][14][19] Compared to majority voting, weighted aggregation can extract more value from limited diversity, especially when some models are systematically stronger or weaker.

### 3.3 Structured judge or aggregator models

A more recent trend is to use a dedicated *judge* or *aggregator* LLM that consumes the outputs of multiple experts and synthesizes them into a final answer. This paradigm appears both in evaluation settings (LLM-as-a-judge) and in multi-agent councils for generation and reasoning.[2][9][11] The key idea is that instead of predefining rules for combining expert outputs, one uses another model to reason about the content, detect hallucinations or inconsistencies, and produce a structured, coherent output.

Council Mode’s consensus synthesis phase is a prime example.[11] After an intelligent triage step and parallel generation by heterogeneous experts, a dedicated synthesis model receives the original query, all expert responses, and metadata, and produces an output organized into four sections: consensus points, disagreements, unique findings, and a comprehensive analysis.[11] This structured synthesis is more than simple voting; it explicitly identifies where experts agree, where they diverge, and which contributions are unique, and then uses this to construct a final answer. In quantitative evaluations, Council Mode achieves a 35.9% relative reduction in hallucination rates on HaluEval and a 7.8-point improvement on TruthfulQA compared to the best individual model, while also reducing bias variance by roughly 85–89%.[11] Importantly, replacing this structured synthesis with simple majority voting increases the hallucination rate by 32.7%, underscoring the value of an intelligent aggregator.[11]

The llm-council architecture, described as a distributed multi-agent consensus system, follows a similar three-stage pattern: first opinions, peer review, and final synthesis.[2] In the final stage, a designated “chairman” model receives the original query, all candidate responses from the first stage, and the reviews and rankings from the second stage, and then performs a map-reduce style reduction to a single high-quality answer.[2] The authors note that this mirrors human decision-making structures such as parliaments or scientific reviews and is particularly recommended for high-risk tasks like medical diagnosis or legal analysis.[2] Although most examples focus on open-ended generation, the architecture readily extends to structured extraction if the chairman is instructed to output JSON or another structured schema.

In the evaluation domain, LLM-as-a-judge frameworks use one LLM to score or compare outputs from other models, often in pairwise or listwise ranking formats.[9] While this is framed as evaluation rather than inference-time extraction, it highlights how a judge can learn nuanced quality assessments that go beyond simple correctness, such as coherence, factuality, or safety. Recent dependence-aware label aggregation methods also show that modeling the dependence among multiple LLM judges improves the reliability of aggregated evaluations.[10] These ideas can be repurposed for structured extraction councils by having a judge model both adjudicate conflicting extractions and estimate confidence scores for downstream routing or human review.

For structured data extraction specifically, the OpenAI “Structured Outputs for Multi-Agent Systems” cookbook illustrates how JSON-mode and strict schema enforcement can be used to orchestrate multi-agent systems, including a triaging agent, preprocessing agent, analysis agent, and visualization agent.[16] While this example focuses on data analysis and visualization rather than document extraction, it demonstrates how a central controller can enforce schemas and orchestrate multiple specialized agents, a pattern that is directly transferable to extraction councils where a judge must output structured fields.

The main advantage of judge models over majority or weighted voting is their ability to reason about the *content* of extractions, incorporate domain knowledge, and use natural-language justification to detect implausible fields. For example, a judge can note that a date extracted as “2099” is inconsistent with the document’s context, even if multiple experts proposed it. However, judge models also introduce new risks: if the judge shares the same biases and blind spots as the experts, its synthesis may reify a false consensus. Dependence-aware modeling and diversity among both experts and judges can mitigate this, but the issue underscores the importance of careful selection and evaluation of judge models.[8][10][11]

### 3.4 Self-consistency and multi-sampling for extraction

Self-consistency is another ensemble technique that operates not across different models but across multiple sampled reasoning paths of the same model. The original self-consistency method for chain-of-thought prompting proposes sampling multiple diverse reasoning paths and selecting the most consistent answer across them, replacing naive greedy decoding in reasoning tasks.[7] Confidence-augmented self-consistency further shows that combining self-consistency with explicit confidence estimation can enhance performance on reasoning benchmarks.[18] These methods have been particularly successful for arithmetic and commonsense reasoning tasks, where the final answer is relatively low-dimensional and can be voted on across sampled chains.[7][18]

For structured data extraction, self-consistency can in principle be adapted by sampling multiple extractions from the same model and aggregating them, analogous to majority voting across runs. However, several practical considerations arise. Structured extraction often requires deterministic, reproducible outputs for downstream systems, and repeated sampling may introduce unnecessary variability. Moreover, many extraction models are prompted or fine-tuned to be deterministic at low temperature, meaning that self-consistency would require reintroducing randomness via higher temperatures or prompt variation, which might increase noise and false positives.

There is currently limited direct empirical evidence on self-consistency specifically for structured extraction tasks. Most published results focus on reasoning benchmarks rather than document fields. Nevertheless, one can extrapolate cautiously: self-consistency is likely to be most useful in extraction subtasks that involve nontrivial reasoning or implicit calculation, such as computing durations, aggregating numeric values across a document, or inferring implicit attributes. In such settings, multiple sampled reasoning paths could indeed help reduce errors by averaging out unstable intermediate steps, especially if combined with confidence estimates for each candidate extraction.[18] For straightforward span extraction or classification, however, the overhead of multi-sampling may not be justified relative to cross-model ensembles or cascades.

Given the current evidence, self-consistency should be viewed as a complementary technique that can be integrated within a single model’s internal deliberation rather than a primary mechanism for cross-model councils in structured extraction. Its greatest value may arise when a cheaper base model is used with self-consistency to avoid escalating to expensive models, or when self-consistency is used to generate a set of candidate extractions that are then evaluated by a judge or weighted aggregation mechanism.

### 3.5 Comparative assessment for structured extraction

Bringing these strands together, one can compare consensus mechanisms along several axes: empirical performance, robustness to correlated errors, data and compute requirements, and suitability for structured extraction.

Majority voting is conceptually simple, easy to implement, and can provide meaningful gains when ensemble members are reasonably diverse and outputs are carefully normalized.[3] For structured extraction tasks that resemble classification—such as choosing an attribute from a small set of possible values—majority voting across models or prompts can be a solid baseline, especially when augmented with domain-specific tie-breaking rules and confidence-aware thresholds.[1][3][14] However, its limitations become apparent when models share systematic biases or when the space of possible outputs is large, leading to scattering of votes across many distinct values.

Weighted aggregation, especially when weights are learned from data, consistently outperforms unweighted majority voting in settings where model reliabilities differ.[1][10][13] LLM-ensemble demonstrates this clearly for attribute extraction, achieving superior performance to all individual models by iteratively learning weights for each LLM based on their historical errors.[1] Dependence-aware aggregation further improves over classical methods by modeling correlated errors, which is particularly relevant given the documented error correlations among LLMs.[8][10] For industrial structured extraction systems that have access to labeled data, weighted voting or probabilistic label aggregation is emerging as a best practice.

Judge or aggregator models, as in Council Mode and llm-council, show particularly strong effects on hallucination and bias reduction by leveraging an additional LLM to reason about expert outputs.[2][11] Council Mode’s structured synthesis reduces hallucinations by 35.9% and outperforms simple majority voting, which increases hallucinations by over 30% in their experiments.[11] For structured extraction, the ability of a judge to consider the full document context and cross-check field consistency makes this approach especially powerful, though it is more computationally expensive and requires careful prompt or schema design to ensure the judge enforces structured output.[11][16][19]

Self-consistency, while powerful in reasoning tasks, currently has less direct evidence for structured extraction and should be seen as a secondary tool, mainly for complex fields requiring reasoning, rather than a primary council mechanism.[7][18] It can, however, be integrated into cascades and routing policies to extract maximum value from a single model before invoking multi-model councils.

Overall, the empirical evidence suggests that for structured data extraction, learned or structured aggregation mechanisms—weighted voting guided by data or judge-based consensus with explicit reasoning about disagreements—tend to outperform simple majority voting, particularly in the presence of correlated errors and heterogeneous model performance.[1][10][11][14] Where labeled data is scarce, majority voting supplemented by careful normalization, diversity in model selection, and domain-specific consistency checks remains a reasonable starting point.[3][14]

## 4. Cascades and Model Routing: Accuracy–Cost Tradeoffs in Councils

### 4.1 Cascades versus parallel councils and layered MoA

In many practical applications, the constraint is not just accuracy or robustness but also computational cost. Running several large LLMs on every document is often prohibitive, especially when processing large-scale corpora such as e-commerce catalogs, financial filings, or medical records. This has led to growing interest in *cascades* and *model routing* techniques, which aim to deploy expensive models selectively, only when necessary.[4][5][11][15]

In a pure parallel council, all models are invoked for every query, and their outputs are combined through the consensus mechanisms discussed above.[1][11][14] This maximizes the use of model diversity but also maximizes cost. Cascades, by contrast, process each query through a sequence of models or policies, starting with cheaper or simpler models and escalating to more powerful ones only when the estimated uncertainty or difficulty exceeds a threshold.[4][5][15] In effect, they reallocate computational budget from easy queries, which a small model can solve reliably, to hard ones that require multi-model deliberation.

Layered mixture-of-agents (MoA) architectures represent a hybrid pattern. In MoA, multiple LLM agents are arranged in layers, with each agent in a layer taking as input the outputs of agents in the previous layer in addition to the original query.[6] This allows for iterative refinement and cross-pollination of reasoning, often using models of similar size but diverse configurations. While MoA has been studied mainly for open-ended generation and reasoning, the underlying idea of layered deliberation with shared context is relevant for structured extraction councils that might benefit from multiple rounds of refinement and validation.[6]

### 4.2 FrugalGPT and learned LLM cascades

FrugalGPT is one of the earliest and most influential frameworks for using LLM cascades to reduce inference cost while maintaining or even improving accuracy.[5] The authors conceptualize three types of strategies for reducing LLM costs: prompt adaptation, LLM approximation, and LLM cascade.[5] In the cascade setting, they propose a simple yet flexible instantiation in which the system learns which combinations of LLMs to use for different queries, guided by a training set that includes ground-truth labels and model outputs.[5] The cascade can choose to answer a query using a cheap model alone, escalate to a more powerful model, or combine outputs as needed.

Empirically, FrugalGPT demonstrates substantial efficiency gains. Their experiments show that FrugalGPT can match the performance of the best individual LLM (e.g., GPT-4) with up to 98% cost reduction, or alternatively improve accuracy over GPT-4 by 4% while maintaining the same cost.[5] These results, while obtained primarily on tasks like question answering and summarization, illustrate the potential of learned cascades to deliver both cost and quality benefits. The key is that the cascade policy learns to recognize when cheaper models suffice and when more expensive models are required, avoiding wasteful overuse of large models.

For structured extraction, the analogy is direct. Many document fields are relatively easy to extract—dates, straightforward numeric amounts, simple attributes—and can be handled by smaller models or even deterministic rules.[1][14][19] Complex fields, such as nuanced legal clauses or ambiguous product attributes with subtle context dependencies, may require stronger models or multi-model councils. A FrugalGPT-style cascade can therefore be trained on an extraction dataset, using features such as model confidence, entropy, or disagreement patterns to decide which queries to escalate. Although FrugalGPT does not focus specifically on structured extraction, its core insights have inspired many subsequent cascade designs.

### 4.3 UCCI: calibrated routing for named entity recognition

UCCI extends the cascade paradigm with a particular focus on *calibration* of uncertainty for cost-optimal routing.[4] The authors note that most deployed routers use uncalibrated confidence scores and require per-workload threshold tuning, which can be brittle and suboptimal.[4] To address this, they present UCCI, a calibration-first router that maps token-level margin uncertainty to per-query error probabilities via isotonic regression and then selects an escalation threshold by constrained cost minimization.[4]

In a production setting, UCCI is evaluated on a named entity recognition workload of 75,000 queries served by 4B and 12B instruction-tuned LLMs on H100 GPUs.[4] By calibrating token-level uncertainty into error probabilities and optimizing the escalation threshold under cost constraints, UCCI reduces inference cost by 31% relative to always using the large model, while maintaining a micro-F1 score of 0.91.[4] It also reduces expected calibration error (ECE) from 0.12 to 0.03, and outperforms entropy-based, conformal, and FrugalGPT-style baselines at the same cost.[4]

This work is particularly important because it is directly grounded in a structured extraction task—NER—and demonstrates that carefully calibrated routing can achieve substantial cost savings with minimal loss in accuracy. The use of token-level uncertainty is especially relevant for extraction, where one can aggregate uncertainty over the tokens involved in an entity or field to estimate the probability of error for that extraction. UCCI’s findings also underscore that naïve confidence measures (such as raw probabilities or softmax scores) are insufficient; calibration techniques like isotonic regression are needed to reliably map model outputs to error probabilities that can drive routing decisions.[4]

### 4.4 Budget-constrained policy learning for cascades

Beyond FrugalGPT and UCCI, work on budget-constrained policy learning for contextual cascades explores how to learn policies that respect resource constraints while maintaining performance.[15] In such frameworks, each query can be processed by a sequence of modules (which may be models, retrieval operations, or other computations), and the goal is to maximize task performance subject to a budget on expected cost.[15] Although this line of work predates some LLM-specific innovations, its general principles apply: the cascade policy can be trained via reinforcement learning or cost-sensitive classification to choose, at each step, whether to accept the current output, perform additional computation, or escalate to a more expensive model.

For structured extraction, this suggests designing cascades where the system can decide, field by field, whether the current extraction is reliable enough or whether further steps—such as invoking a stronger LLM, engaging a council, or applying additional validation—are warranted. The budget-constrained perspective emphasizes that such decisions should be learned from data rather than hand-tuned, balancing the marginal gain in accuracy from extra computation against its cost.[4][5][15]

### 4.5 Mixture-of-Agents and layered deliberation

The Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) methodology takes a slightly different approach. Instead of routing queries from smaller to larger models, MoA constructs a layered architecture in which each layer comprises multiple LLM agents, and each agent in a layer takes as input the outputs of agents in the previous layer as auxiliary information.[6] This creates a form of iterative deliberation, where later agents can refine, critique, or build upon earlier outputs. In experiments on benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench, and FLASK, MoA models attain state-of-the-art performance, surpassing even GPT-4 Omni in some settings.[6] For example, a MoA using only open-source LLMs leads AlpacaEval 2.0 with a score of 65.1%, compared to 57.5% for GPT-4 Omni.[6]

While MoA is not focused specifically on structured extraction, its layered design offers insights for councils in such tasks. One can imagine a layered extraction pipeline where the first layer performs coarse extraction, the second layer refines fields and resolves conflicts, and a final layer performs global consistency checks across the document. Each layer could use multiple agents for diversity, and the structured outputs from one layer would serve as inputs for the next, analogous to how MoA agents incorporate previous outputs as auxiliary context.[6] Such designs can be seen as a middle ground between parallel councils and cascades, combining iterative refinement with diversity at each stage.

### 4.6 Accuracy–cost tradeoffs and when cascades win

Across these works, a consistent picture emerges: cascades and routing strategies can substantially reduce cost while maintaining or slightly improving accuracy, especially on workloads where many queries are easy and only a minority require expensive, multi-model deliberation. FrugalGPT demonstrates up to 98% cost reduction at constant performance or modest accuracy gains at equal cost.[5] UCCI achieves a 31% cost reduction on a production NER workload while maintaining micro-F1 of 0.91 and improving calibration.[4] Council Mode’s triage mechanism ensures that only complex queries are sent to the full council, reducing unnecessary expert calls while still achieving large hallucination and bias reductions on the queries that matter.[11]

For structured extraction, these results translate into concrete design guidance. It is often efficient to deploy a small, domain-specialized or distilled model as a first-stage extractor, using calibrated uncertainty estimates or simple heuristic checks to decide when to accept its output and when to escalate.[4][5][19] Difficult cases can then be handled by a more expensive stage, which may involve a single frontier model, a heterogeneous council, or a judge-based consensus mechanism.[1][11][14] Given the empirical evidence, cascades that integrate calibrated uncertainty, learned routing, and explicit budget constraints are more robust and economical than either always using the largest model or always invoking a full council.

However, cascades are not universally superior. When the distribution of query difficulty is narrow—meaning most queries are hard—or when uncertainty estimation is poorly calibrated, the overhead of routing and repeated calls may erode cost savings without delivering commensurate accuracy gains.[4][5] Moreover, cascades introduce additional complexity in system design, monitoring, and failure analysis. For high-stakes extraction tasks, designers often choose to combine cascades with councils: use cascades to filter trivial cases and councils to handle nontrivial ones, ensuring that the most consequential decisions benefit from multi-model deliberation.[11][14][17]

## 5. Scaling the Number of Models: Diminishing Returns and Weak Models

### 5.1 Classical ensemble theory and expectations

Classical ensemble theory suggests that adding more models to an ensemble can improve performance, but only under certain conditions. Each additional model must contribute new information—i.e., its errors should not be perfectly correlated with existing models—and its individual accuracy should be at least slightly better than random for the ensemble to benefit from its inclusion. As the number of models grows, the marginal gain from each additional model typically diminishes, especially if new models are similar to those already included.

In the context of LLM councils, one might hope that simply adding more models will monotonically improve consensus accuracy. However, given the empirical evidence on correlated errors and the heterogeneity in model quality, the reality is more complex. At some point, additional models may add cost without significant accuracy gains, or even degrade performance if their errors are systematically biased and not properly down-weighted.

### 5.2 Empirical findings on the number of experts

Council Mode provides some of the clearest empirical evidence on how the number and diversity of experts affect performance.[11] In their analysis, the authors examine the effect of using different numbers of experts and different ensemble compositions. They find that reducing the number of experts from three to two increases the hallucination rate by 19.6%, suggesting that three experts strike a good balance between diversity and computational cost in their setting.[11] This indicates that there is a tangible benefit to having at least three heterogeneous models in the council, at least for the kinds of tasks evaluated.

At the same time, the authors report that using three instances of the same model—a same-model ensemble—yields only modest improvements over the single model.[11] This aligns with the error correlation findings and suggests that beyond a small number of copies, additional instances of the same model add little value and can be largely redundant. In other words, diversity among experts is more important than sheer count.

The LLM-ensemble work does not focus extensively on the number of models but on learning optimal weights; nevertheless, it implicitly assumes that the ensemble size is fixed and that the key leverage is in weighting rather than simply adding more models.[1] ZeroSemble explicitly chooses three heterogeneous models for its ensemble and does not report additional gains from adding more; instead, the emphasis is on how the two-step pipeline leverages their combined strengths.[14] These design choices suggest that, in practice, ensembles with two to four carefully chosen models are typical, with little evidence that very large councils are needed for structured extraction tasks.

### 5.3 Weak models: help or hindrance?

The role of weak models in ensembles is subtle. On one hand, ensemble learning theory shows that an ensemble of weak learners, when combined in a boosting framework, can produce a strong classifier, as long as each weak learner performs slightly better than chance and diversity is maintained. The AdaBoost-inspired ensemble of weak LLM supervisors for stronger models embodies this principle.[12] In their easy-to-hard (e2h) framework, weak models trained on simpler tasks supervise stronger models on more complex tasks, and the ensemble method improves performance across classification and generative tasks by up to 14% over baseline methods.[12] In several cases, their ensemble approach matches the performance of models trained directly on ground-truth data, illustrating the power of collective supervision from weak models when combined properly.[12]

On the other hand, if weak models are simply added to a council without careful weighting or control, they can drag down consensus accuracy, especially in majority voting schemes where all votes are treated equally. If a weak model consistently mis-extracts certain fields or exhibits systematic biases, its votes can tilt the ensemble in the wrong direction, particularly when stronger models are in disagreement or when the number of weak models outnumbers strong ones. This is exacerbated when weak models’ errors are correlated with each other, which is likely if they share architectures or training data.[8][10]

Dependence-aware label aggregation methods address this by estimating each annotator’s reliability and their dependence structure, thereby limiting the influence of weak and correlated annotators.[10] In a multi-LLM council, similar techniques can ensure that weak models do not unduly influence consensus decisions. Weighted aggregation methods like LLM-ensemble also naturally down-weight models that have historically underperformed on specific attributes, protecting the ensemble from their errors.[1] In the absence of such weighting, however, the folklore that “more models are always better” is not supported by evidence.

### 5.4 Diminishing returns and practical ensemble sizes

The combination of empirical evidence and theoretical considerations suggests that the benefits of adding more models to a council are subject to diminishing returns and can even reverse if weak or correlated models are added without proper weighting. Council Mode’s results indicate that a small ensemble of three heterogeneous experts provides substantial gains over a single model, while moving from three to larger numbers is not reported to yield commensurate benefits.[11] ZeroSemble’s successful use of three models and LLM-ensemble’s focus on a finite set of LLMs similarly support modest ensemble sizes.[1][14]

In practice, the primary constraints on ensemble size are computational cost, latency, and engineering complexity. Each additional model increases inference cost and system complexity, particularly when models are hosted on different platforms or require different orchestration strategies.[2][5][11] Given that error correlation among strong models is high, the marginal value of each new model quickly shrinks, especially when models come from the same provider or share architectures.[8]

A pragmatic best practice emerging from current work is to select a small set of two to four diverse models that complement each other’s strengths, and then invest in sophisticated aggregation—weighted voting, dependence-aware label aggregation, or judge-based consensus—rather than expanding the ensemble arbitrarily.[1][10][11][14] Weak models can be included if their roles are carefully defined, such as providing cheap initial extractions in a cascade, generating candidate fields for stronger models to validate, or contributing to a boosting-like training regime, but they should not be given equal voting power in final consensus unless their reliability has been empirically verified.[5][12]

## 6. Handling Disagreement, Ties, and Systemic Biases

### 6.1 Tie-breaking and disagreement resolution strategies

In any multi-LLM council, disagreements among models are inevitable. Handling these disagreements robustly is as important as designing the ensemble itself. For structured extraction, disagreements can manifest as different values for the same field, different spans for entities, or different interpretations of ambiguous document sections. Designing principled tie-breaking and resolution strategies is therefore a central part of council design.

The majority voting guide emphasizes that one common pitfall is failing to define clear tie-breaking rules, leading to ad hoc or inconsistent behavior when votes are split or when multiple responses receive similar support.[3] The recommended remedies include using secondary signals such as model confidence, requesting additional samples, or escalating to human review when consensus is weak.[3] For structured extraction, one can also leverage domain-specific constraints: for example, if two values are tied but one violates a schema constraint or falls outside a plausible numeric range, the other can be preferred.

Judge-based consensus architectures handle disagreements by design. In llm-council, the chairman model receives not only the initial opinions but also peer reviews and rankings of those opinions.[2] This rich context allows the chairman to weigh arguments, assess critiques, and synthesize a decision that may align with neither the simple majority nor any single expert. Council Mode’s synthesis model explicitly organizes expert outputs into consensus points, disagreements, and unique findings, and then uses this structure to justify its final answer.[11] When faced with ties or conflicting extractions, the synthesis model is instructed to explain which evidence supports each candidate and to choose the best justified one, often based on coherence with the document and cross-field consistency.[11]

In structured extraction, similar patterns can be applied. The judge model can be prompted to compare candidate field values, reference the original document text, and consider which candidate best matches the context. For example, if one candidate extraction for “interest rate” appears in a header and another in a footnote, the judge can be instructed to select the one in the main terms section, reflecting domain knowledge. Ties can thus be broken not by vote counts alone but by reasoning about provenance and semantics.

### 6.2 Dependence-aware aggregation and annotator modeling

Traditional label aggregation methods treat annotators as independent sources of noisy labels whose errors are conditionally independent given the true label. However, this assumption is often violated: annotators may share training, backgrounds, or biases, leading to correlated errors. The dependence-aware label aggregation framework addresses this by modeling dependencies among annotators, capturing patterns of co-agreement and disagreement beyond what would be expected by chance.[10] The authors demonstrate that their method improves aggregation performance over classical baselines on multiple real-world datasets, highlighting the importance of accounting for dependence.[10]

For LLM councils, this perspective is particularly relevant given the documented correlated errors among models.[8] Each model can be treated as an annotator, and their joint predictions across a labeled dataset can be used to estimate not only individual confusion matrices but also dependence structures. For example, two models from the same provider may be strongly positively correlated, while a smaller open-source model might have more independent errors. Dependence-aware aggregation can then adjust the effective weight of each model’s votes, reducing the undue influence of groups of correlated models that share blind spots.[10]

The One-Coin Dawid–Skene (OCDS) model used in crowdsourced annotation pipelines provides a simpler example of how annotator reliability can be estimated and used to improve aggregation. In an evaluation of basic and advanced interfaces for crowdsourced labels, an advanced One-Coin Dawid–Skene model achieved accuracies of around 81.5%, outperforming simple majority voting.[13] While this study involves human annotators rather than LLMs, the principle carries over: even a simple per-annotator reliability model can yield better aggregation than unweighted voting, and more sophisticated dependence-aware models can go further.[10][13]

In structured extraction systems, incorporating such label aggregation techniques means using historical extraction data to estimate per-model reliabilities and correlations, then using these estimates to compute posterior probabilities over field values. Instead of picking the most common value, the system would pick the value with the highest posterior probability under the model, given the observed votes and the estimated reliability and dependence structure. This provides a principled way to handle disagreements and mitigate the impact of weak or correlated models.

### 6.3 Pitfalls: false consensus and shared biases

Perhaps the most insidious pitfall in multi-LLM councils is the risk of *false consensus* arising from shared biases and correlated errors. When multiple models from similar families or providers are queried on the same input, their agreement may be taken as evidence of correctness, even when they are all wrong.[3][8] The correlated errors study quantifies this risk: high agreement among models is often observed precisely when they are making the same mistake, due to shared training data or architectures.[8] This can create a dangerous illusion of safety, particularly in high-stakes settings such as hiring, medical decision support, or financial risk assessment.[8][17]

In LLM-as-a-judge settings, where one model is used to proxy ground truth labels, correlated errors can lead to overestimation of model performance. The study shows that judges tend to overinflate the accuracy of models that share their architecture or provider, especially when evaluating models that are in fact less accurate.[8] This has direct implications for councils: if both the experts and the judge share biases, the entire system may converge on and validate incorrect extractions.

Council Mode explicitly targets this problem by using heterogeneous models and a structured synthesis protocol that surfaces disagreements and unique findings.[11] By explicitly identifying where experts diverge and preserving minority viewpoints, the system reduces the likelihood that a false consensus will be accepted unexamined.[11] The authors note that using three instances of the same model yields only modest improvements, while heterogeneous councils dramatically reduce hallucinations and bias variance.[11] ZeroSemble’s reliance on heterogeneous models rather than multiple copies of a single model reflects a similar concern with algorithmic monoculture.[14]

The majority voting guide also warns that using homogeneous ensemble members is a common mistake that leads to correlated failures.[3] It recommends diversifying models, prompt templates, or sampling parameters to reduce shared blind spots and cautions that majority voting should not be relied upon for highly subjective tasks where biases are strong.[3] For structured extraction, the analog is to avoid councils composed solely of models from one provider or training paradigm, especially when extracting fields where societal biases may be salient, such as occupation, gendered language, or demographic attributes.

### 6.4 Reliability, hallucination, and bias in high-stakes extraction

Multi-LLM councils are increasingly proposed for high-stakes domains such as clinical decision support and financial reporting, where structured extraction of critical data points is central.[17][19] In clinical contexts, consensus-based ensemble approaches that leverage multiple open models have been discussed as a way to improve reliability of AI decision support systems, including multi-agent clinical decision frameworks where different models extract and cross-validate clinical data before recommendations are made.[17] The stakes in such domains make the pitfalls of false consensus and biased extraction particularly acute.

Council Mode’s evaluation demonstrates that multi-agent consensus can substantially reduce hallucination and bias variance compared to single models, but it also underscores that these gains hinge on architectural diversity and structured synthesis.[11] For structured extraction, designers of clinical or financial systems often add further safeguards: rule-based validation, hard constraints derived from regulations, and human-in-the-loop review for flagged discrepancies.[17][19] Multi-LLM councils are thus one layer in a broader safety architecture, not a panacea.

The key empirical lesson is that while councils generally improve reliability, they do not eliminate systemic biases or correlated errors, especially when the pool of frontier models is itself biased or monocultural.[8][11][17] As such, best practices emphasize combining diverse models, sophisticated aggregation that accounts for dependence, domain-specific validation, and selective human oversight for high-risk fields. This layered approach is essential for mitigating the risk of false consensus in structured data extraction.

## 7. Design Patterns and Evidence-Based Recommendations

### 7.1 Architectural patterns for extraction pipelines

Concrete designs for structured extraction councils typically integrate the ideas discussed above into end-to-end pipelines. A common pattern begins with document ingestion, parsing, and preprocessing, followed by one or more LLM-based extraction stages, and culminating in aggregation, validation, and storage of structured outputs.[14][19] Multi-LLM councils can be embedded in the extraction stages to enhance robustness and in the validation stages to adjudicate conflicts.

ZeroSemble provides a template for such a pipeline at the document level. Raw documents are first processed to segment text and identify candidate entities.[14] Three heterogeneous LLMs are then invoked to perform entity extraction, and their outputs are combined to produce a high-confidence entity set, privileging entities supported by multiple models.[14] This set is then used to constrain relation extraction, ensuring that only relations involving high-confidence entities are considered.[14] The resulting structured information is thus the product of both model diversity and structured post-processing.

The multi-agent financial KPI system offers another concrete example. Documents such as 10-Ks, 8-Ks, and earnings transcripts are ingested and parsed into logical sections, with OCR applied as needed.[19] Text is normalized, and tables and numeric spans are detected.[19] Domain-specific embedding models identify segments likely to contain KPI-relevant information, which are then passed to an extraction agent that uses LLMs to extract KPIs, fiscal periods, and metadata, subject to rule-based validation.[19] A subsequent text-to-SQL agent translates analyst queries into executable SQL over the extracted KPI database.[19] While this system does not yet employ a full multi-LLM council at each step, it demonstrates how modular, multi-agent architectures can be used for structured extraction, and it could be extended to incorporate model ensembles or councils in the extraction and validation agents.

In an LLM-ensemble-style architecture for e-commerce attributes, multiple models are invoked in parallel for each product, and their attribute predictions are aggregated using learned weights.[1] The resulting attributes are then passed through safety filters and business logic before being deployed in production catalogs.[1] Cascades or triage mechanisms could further optimize this pipeline by using cheaper models for straightforward attributes and reserving multi-model ensembles for attributes known to be challenging.

### 7.2 Choosing and measuring model diversity

When assembling a council for structured extraction, the evidence strongly supports prioritizing architectural and provider diversity to reduce correlated errors.[8][11][14] Models from different providers, trained on different data and using different architectures, are more likely to make independent mistakes, increasing the potential benefit from aggregation.[8][11] Where possible, including both frontier proprietary models and strong open-source models can enhance diversity, as can including models fine-tuned on different domains or tasks.

Quantitatively, diversity can be assessed by evaluating candidate models on a validation set and computing pairwise error agreement rates: the fraction of examples where both models are wrong and agree on the same incorrect label.[8] Models with high error agreement offer less marginal benefit when combined. Additional metrics such as Cohen’s kappa, mutual information between predictions and ground truth, or clustering of models based on confusion patterns can help identify groups of models that are too similar to warrant inclusion in the same council.

In unlabeled settings, diversity can be approximated by measuring how often models’ normalized outputs differ. For structured extraction, where outputs are discrete fields, this is relatively straightforward: fields that are frequently disagreed upon indicate potential difficulty or model heterogeneity. However, unlabeled diversity measures must be interpreted cautiously, as differences in representation do not always correspond to differences in correctness.

Overall, the empirical evidence argues against simply combining as many strong models as possible. Instead, it supports a deliberate selection of a small number of complementary models whose error patterns are as decorrelated as possible, informed by validation data and diversity metrics.[8][11][14]

### 7.3 Selecting a consensus mechanism by task type

Different consensus mechanisms are better suited to different types of structured extraction. For simple classification-style tasks—such as assigning a category, sentiment, or discrete attribute—majority voting across heterogeneous models can be an effective baseline, especially when combined with normalization and tie-breaking rules informed by domain constraints.[1][3] Weighted voting or probabilistic label aggregation further improves performance when individual model reliabilities differ and can be estimated from data.[1][10][13]

For more complex extraction tasks involving multiple fields, spans, and relations across a document, judge-based consensus models become increasingly attractive. A judge can reason about the coherence of the entire structured output, enforcing consistency across fields (for example, ensuring that extracted dates and amounts align with each other and with textual descriptions) and rejecting implausible combinations.[11][16][19] The strong performance of Council Mode’s structured synthesis, which significantly reduces hallucination rates compared to both single models and simple majority voting, supports the value of such judge-based approaches.[11] When combined with JSON or schema-enforced outputs, judge models can generate well-structured extraction results even in the face of conflicting expert outputs.[16]

Self-consistency and multi-sampling are most relevant when extraction involves substantial reasoning. For instance, extracting an implied end date from a contract based on a start date and duration, or inferring a derived KPI that requires combining several reported metrics, may benefit from self-consistent reasoning across multiple sampled chains.[7][18] However, for basic span detection or direct attribute extraction, cross-model ensembles and cascades provide more proven value.

Ultimately, consensus mechanism choice should be informed by empirical evaluation on the target workload. LLM-ensemble and ZeroSemble demonstrate that weighted and structured aggregation can be learned from data to outperform simpler methods.[1][14] Council Mode shows that structured synthesis can dramatically reduce hallucinations and bias.[11] These works collectively support the conclusion that while majority voting is a useful baseline, advanced aggregation methods are preferable for complex structured extraction tasks where labeled data is available and stakes are high.

### 7.4 Integrating cascades, councils, and self-consistency

The most effective systems often combine cascades with councils, applying different mechanisms at different stages of processing. A typical pattern for structured extraction might proceed as follows. A small or distilled model performs initial extraction, benefiting from its low cost and speed. Its outputs are accompanied by calibrated uncertainty estimates or heuristic confidence scores, possibly derived via methods like UCCI’s token-level margin calibration.[4] For fields with low estimated error probability, the system accepts the small model’s output. For fields with higher error probability or where domain constraints are violated, the system escalates.

Escalation may involve invoking a larger frontier model for the field, running a heterogeneous council of models with weighted or judge-based aggregation, or performing self-consistency sampling with a stronger model.[4][5][11][14] In high-stakes cases, the system might route the outputs of the council to a human reviewer, highlighting disagreements and key evidence extracted from the document. Self-consistency could be used within the large model to generate multiple candidate extractions for a judge to evaluate, particularly for fields requiring reasoning.[7][18]

This integrated approach leverages the strengths of different techniques: cascades minimize cost by reserving expensive computation for difficult cases; councils provide robustness and bias reduction through diversity and aggregation; and self-consistency enhances reasoning in targeted fields. The successes of FrugalGPT, UCCI, Council Mode, and domain-specific pipelines like financial KPI extraction collectively demonstrate that such hybrid architectures can achieve high accuracy and reliability at reasonable cost.[4][5][11][19]

### 7.5 Evaluation methodology and open questions

Designing multi-LLM councils for structured extraction also requires robust evaluation methodologies. Labeled datasets are essential for estimating model reliabilities, calibrating uncertainties, and learning ensemble weights or routing policies.[1][4][5][10][14][19] Evaluation should be conducted not only at the field level but also at the document level, assessing the coherence and completeness of the entire structured output. Metrics such as micro- and macro-F1, calibration error, and bias variance are relevant, as illustrated by UCCI and Council Mode.[4][11]

LLM-as-a-judge frameworks can be used to augment human evaluation, but their susceptibility to correlated errors and shared biases must be accounted for.[8][9][10] Dependence-aware label aggregation offers a way to integrate judgments from multiple LLM judges while modeling their dependencies, but the ground truth for structured extraction tasks remains critical for reliable benchmarking.[10]

Several open questions remain. First, more work is needed specifically on self-consistency for structured extraction, as most existing evidence comes from reasoning tasks. Second, the space of possible judge models and prompt designs for structured consensus is vast, and best practices for making judges robust and unbiased are still emerging.[9][11][16] Third, systematic studies are needed to quantify the tradeoffs between parallel councils and layered MoA architectures for complex extraction tasks, especially in domains with rich schemas and cross-field constraints.[6][14][19] Finally, the broader issue of algorithmic monoculture—how increasing convergence among top-tier models affects the effectiveness of councils over time—remains an area of concern and investigation.[8]

## Conclusion

The current literature from 2024 to 2026 provides a rich and increasingly evidence-based foundation for designing multi-LLM councils, ensembles, and consensus systems for structured data extraction. For the user’s specific questions, several clear themes emerge.

Regarding model diversity and decorrelation of errors, empirical work shows that LLM errors are substantially correlated, especially among models that are more accurate, share providers, or share architectures.[8] Heterogeneous ensembles that mix models from different families and providers do reduce correlated mistakes relative to homogeneous ensembles, and systems like ZeroSemble and Council Mode leverage this diversity to improve document-level extraction and reduce hallucination and bias.[11][14] However, independence is partial at best; as models improve, they converge not only in accuracy but also in their blind spots, underscoring the need for additional safeguards and diversity beyond model count alone.[8]

On the choice of consensus mechanisms, simple majority voting is a practical baseline that can improve consistency and reduce random errors in structured extraction when outputs are normalized and ensemble members are diversified.[1][3][14] Yet, in the presence of correlated errors and heterogeneous model reliabilities, weighted voting and learned aggregation methods such as LLM-ensemble and dependence-aware label aggregation perform better, particularly when trained on labeled data.[1][10][13] Judge-based consensus models, exemplified by Council Mode and llm-council, offer the most powerful tools for complex extraction tasks, using LLMs to reason about disagreements and content to reduce hallucination and bias; replacing such structured synthesis with simple majority voting significantly worsens hallucination rates.[2][11] Self-consistency is clearly beneficial for reasoning tasks but has less direct evidence for structured extraction; it is best viewed as a complementary technique for fields that require reasoning rather than a primary council mechanism.[7][18]

For cascades and model routing, frameworks like FrugalGPT and UCCI demonstrate that learned cascades and calibrated routers can drastically reduce inference costs while maintaining accuracy, including in production structured extraction workloads such as NER.[4][5] Cascades that route easy queries to small models and escalate hard ones to larger or multiple models, often guided by calibrated uncertainty estimates, are emerging as a best practice for cost-efficient structured extraction.[4][5][11] Layered MoA architectures offer an alternative that emphasizes iterative refinement and cross-agent reasoning, with strong performance in general LLM benchmarks, and they suggest promising directions for multi-stage extraction pipelines.[6]

On whether adding more models monotonically helps, both theory and empirical results indicate diminishing returns and the risk of harm from weak or correlated models. Small ensembles of two to four heterogeneous models, combined with sophisticated aggregation, tend to be more effective and economical than large councils of similar models.[1][8][11][14] Weak models can contribute positively in boosting-like frameworks or cascades when their roles and weights are carefully controlled, but naive majority voting that gives equal weight to weak and strong models can degrade performance.[12] Consequently, best practice focuses on careful model selection for diversity and on weighting schemes that reflect empirical reliability and dependence.

Finally, resolving ties and disagreements and avoiding false consensus require conscious design. Simple tie-breaking based on model confidence or additional sampling can help, but judge-based consensus and dependence-aware aggregation provide more principled solutions.[2][10][11] The risk that multiple models share the same bias and thus form an incorrect consensus is real and empirically documented, particularly among models from the same provider or architecture.[3][8] Heterogeneous councils, structured synthesis that surfaces and reasons about disagreements, domain-specific validation, and, where appropriate, human oversight collectively mitigate these risks, especially in high-stakes domains like healthcare and finance.[11][17][19]

Overall, the evidence supports a set of concrete best practices for structured data extraction councils. Use a small number of heterogeneous models with demonstrably decorrelated error patterns. Combine their outputs using weighted or judge-based aggregation rather than relying solely on majority voting, especially for complex, multi-field extractions. Employ cascades and calibrated routing to control costs, escalating to councils only when uncertainty or disagreement is high. Treat weak models as tools for pre-processing or boosting, not as equal partners in final consensus unless their reliability is validated. And continuously monitor for correlated errors and biases, using dependence-aware aggregation, diversity metrics, and domain-specific validation to avoid false consensus. These practices, grounded in the current literature, distinguish empirically supported design choices from folklore and provide a robust foundation for building multi-LLM councils for structured data extraction.
