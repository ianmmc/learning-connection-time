*In district-level education-equity metrics (e.g. student-teacher ratio or instructional-time-per-student), what is current best practice for handling charter schools: include them as pseudo-districts, exclude them, or segment/report them separately? What are the known distortions when charter students and charter staff are mis-partitioned relative to a parent district's enrollment and staffing counts? Cite methodological guidance from NCES, Urban Institute, Center for Learner Equity, or peer-reviewed sources.*

Current practice splits along exactly the fault line you'd expect: what NCES itself does structurally, versus what downstream researchers do when they need a clean "district" denominator.

**NCES's own default is segmentation, not exclusion.** CCD treats every charter LEA — single-school (structure a) or multi-school (b) — as its own agency, distinct from "regular school districts." NCES's Digest convention for "regular school districts" explicitly excludes independent charter agencies along with service agencies and state/federally operated agencies, and NCES's 2022–23 LEA structure data table separately labels "regular school districts, independent charter school districts, supervisory union administrative centers, service agencies, state agencies, federal agencies" as distinct LEA types. The Urban Institute's Education Data Portal mirrors this by exposing a `charter` filter so a call for "the enrollment of charter schools in the District of Columbia" is `?charter=1&fips=11` rather than something baked silently into a district total. When outside researchers build district rankings from CCD, they follow the same logic: one widely used methodology explicitly excludes "agencies where all associated schools are charter schools" from its district comparison set to keep the denominator clean.

So for structures (a) and (b), "pseudo-district" isn't really a choice you make — that's just what a charter LEA *is* in CCD, and best practice is to let it stand as its own unit rather than folding it into a nearby traditional district's numbers. The actual judgment call is structure (c): dependent charters that share a host district's LEAID. Best practice there, per the one applied methodology I found that tackles this head-on, is to pull the dependent charter's schools out of the host LEA's aggregate before computing district-level ratios — not leave them commingled.

**The distortions, concretely:**

- **Numerator/denominator mismatch on cost- and staffing-ratio metrics.** The clearest documented case is from California, where school districts are commingled with charter schools in the master data set, charter schools are associated with the districts that authorize them rather than the organization that runs them, and charter management organizations that create and manage schools across districts aren't recognized as the "parent" for the school of record. This directly inflates ratio metrics: because a district's revenue is reported in full for both district-governed and charter-governed schools, while the student count for that district excludes charter-school students, authorizing districts appear to receive more revenue per student than they actually do. The same mechanism inflates staffing/instructional-time-per-student metrics whenever charter FTE or instructional minutes are dropped from the numerator but the district's nominal enrollment isn't correspondingly adjusted, or vice versa.

- **Fixed-cost / "stranded cost" distortion for the host district's remaining students.** When students leave a district for a charter (especially structure a/b, spun off as independent LEAs), the district's fixed costs — buildings, central administration — don't shrink proportionally. These are called "stranded costs" — expenses that don't go down just because a few kids leave, so local districts end up paying higher costs per pupil. Ladd and Singleton (2019) found a fiscal externality in excess of $500 per traditional public school pupil in their urban North Carolina district, and Bifulco's analysis of Buffalo and Albany found net fiscal impacts of $633–$744 and $976–$1,070 per student respectively. The mechanism is structurally identical for instructional-time metrics: if enrollment drops but staffing/scheduling can't be right-sized immediately, a naive ratio will show artificial improvement for the shrinking side and artificial strain wherever the charter absorbs students without matching capacity. (Note this fiscal-impact literature isn't methodologically uncontested — CRPE has published critiques arguing that attributing all district enrollment loss to charter growth ignores demographic change and other causes of decline, so treat magnitude estimates as directional, not precise.)

- **Compositional confound baked into the LEA-status categorization itself.** Whether a charter lands in structure (a) versus (c) is a matter of state law and local choice, not educational practice, and it correlates with who the school serves. About 54 percent of charter schools were their own independent LEA while 46 percent were part of a traditional LEA, and charter schools operating as their own LEA had a higher percentage of students with disabilities (11.5 percent) than charter schools that were part of an LEA (9.7 percent) — both still below the traditional public school rate of 12.5 percent. If you're building an equity metric and don't control for this, "independent-LEA charter" vs. "dependent charter" isn't a neutral technical split — it's already correlated with enrollment composition.

- **Cross-state/cross-district non-comparability.** A charter school may be classified as its own LEA or as part of a traditional LEA depending on state law, and in some states the charter or its authorizer is permitted to choose, with variation documented state-by-state in CLE's LEA-status table. That means the same charter operator could show up as structure (a) in one state and structure (c) in another for reasons having nothing to do with the school's actual staffing or scheduling — a serious threat to comparability in any multi-state pipeline.

**Bottom line for your LCT formula specifically:** since LCT = Daily Instructional Minutes × Instructional Staff / Enrollment, the failure mode to guard against is exactly the CCD structural ambiguity from your earlier question — a dependent charter's enrollment and instructional minutes need to be pulled cleanly out of (or explicitly kept inside, consistently) the host LEA's aggregate before you compute a ratio, and the choice needs to be applied uniformly across your dataset rather than inherited passively from whatever each state happened to report. NCES's own convention — segment charter LEAs as distinct units, and treat dependent charters as requiring explicit sub-extraction — is the closest thing to a citable federal standard; nobody I found publishes a peer-reviewed consensus method for the dependent-charter extraction step itself, so that part is closer to applied best practice (Rees & Perry) than settled methodology.

*For U.S. charter schools that are NOT their own LEA but are reported within a traditional school district's LEA in NCES CCD, is there a reliable method to separate the charter's instructional staff (ccd_lea_059 / ccd_sch_059) and enrollment (ccd_sch_052) from the host district's totals? Does school-level staffing data (ccd_sch_059) exist and is it complete enough to compute per-school student-teacher ratios? What are the coverage/quality caveats?*

Short answer: yes, there's a reliable method, but it has to be built bottom-up from the school file, not by subtracting from the LEA total — because the LEA aggregate in CCD doesn't publish a charter-only subtotal anywhere. And the staffing side has a real, documented quality caveat that the enrollment side mostly doesn't.

**The extraction method.** The LEA Universe Survey (ccd_lea) reports a single "number of full-time-equivalent teachers" for the whole agency — traditional schools and any dependent charters combined, with instructional-staff and support-staff fields broken out by category but not by charter status. There's no LEA-level "charter carve-out" field. So the only path is: filter ccd_sch to the host LEAID, keep the rows where the charter flag (CHARTER_TEXT/CHARTR) = Yes, and sum FTE teacher count (ccd_sch_059) and membership (ccd_sch_052) across just those campuses. Join on the 12-digit NCESSCH, not name-matching. If you want a "traditional-schools-only" residual for the host district, subtract that charter subtotal from the LEA total rather than trying to find a pre-computed non-charter figure, because none exists.

**Does school-level staffing exist and is it usable?** Yes — FTE classroom teacher count has been a core variable on the Public Elementary/Secondary School Universe Survey since CCD's inception, and it's submitted via EDFacts as FS059 – Staff FTE, distinct from FS052 – Membership. So the fields you're asking about are real and populated in the modern public files.

The completeness caveat is more serious than a typical missing-data problem, though. NCES's own SY 2012–13 documentation describes a specific, recurring editing intervention: in years where SEAs report *more* FTE teachers summed across a district's schools than the LEA-level total, and the SEA can't explain or correct the discrepancy, NCES edits the school-level FTE data to match the LEA-level figure by proportionally adjusting the counts. In plain terms: some published school-level FTE values aren't independently verified headcounts — they're back-calculated shares of the district total, redistributed pro rata to make the arithmetic close. That's precisely the scenario your dependent-charter carve-out depends on being accurate, so it's worth treating as a live risk rather than a footnote: a charter campus's FTE figure could in some state-years be a proportional allocation rather than a real count.

A few other things worth checking before trusting a school-level ratio:

- **Staff category granularity.** The LEA file separates instructional staff from support staff; the school-level file's headline FTE field is specifically "classroom teacher" count, which in some CCD years explicitly includes special-education and other specialist teachers even though those same staff are usually excluded from class-size calculations elsewhere. Confirm which definition your extract is using before comparing across schools or years.
- **FTE ceiling conventions vary by state.** Some states cap an individual teacher's FTE at 1.0; others allow it to exceed 1.0 for staff split across assignments. This affects both within-state summation and cross-state comparability of any ratio you compute.
- **No shared-staff flag.** CCD does carry a "shared-time school" flag for *students*, but there's no equivalent flag for staff shared across a co-located host school and dependent charter. If they share counselors, special-ed staff, or itinerant specialists administratively, CCD gives you no way to detect double-counting or omission from the public file alone.
- **Item nonresponse and missing-value coding.** Not every state reports every data group for every entity every year; values are coded M (expected but missing) vs. N (not applicable) rather than left blank, so a naive sum will silently miscount unless you check for these codes first.
- **Revision lag.** EDFacts collections stay open for SEA revisions for roughly three years after initial release, with NCES issuing a "final" version afterward — so early/provisional-year files for a given campus may still change.

**Enrollment (ccd_sch_052) is the more trustworthy half of the pair.** CCD's coverage of regular public schools — which includes charters — is documented as virtually 100 percent, and because membership counts underlie state and federal funding formulas, compliance incentives are stronger than for staffing. The caveat there is conceptual rather than a coverage gap: FS052/ccd_sch_052 is a point-in-time membership count (a specific fall date), not average daily membership or attendance, so if you're cross-referencing against a state's own per-pupil funding figures, confirm you're comparing the same enrollment concept.

**Practical recommendation:** after extracting the charter's campuses, reconcile — sum FTE and enrollment across every school under the host LEAID (charter and non-charter) and check that it equals the LEA-level total. If it doesn't, or if it matches suspiciously exactly in a way that suggests proportional redistribution, that's your signal to flag the record as an editing-derived rather than an independently reported figure, and, where it matters, verify against the state SEA's own school-level staffing report if one exists — several states publish more granular or more current staffing data than what has propagated through the federal EDFacts pipeline.
